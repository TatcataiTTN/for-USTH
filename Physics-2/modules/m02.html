Electric field of point charges and field lines

$\vec E=\vec F/q_0$; a point charge gives $E=k|q|/r^2$ pointing away from $+$ and toward $-$; fields superpose.

Key formulas
$E=\dfrac{k|q|}{r^2}$, $\ \vec F=q\vec E$, field lines start on $+$ and end on $-$, denser where $E$ is larger

How it is asked: Field at a point from two charges; Where is the net field zero.

Electric field of point charges and field lines: method and traps

Method
  1. Field of each charge at the point: magnitude $k|q|/r^2$, direction away/toward.
  2. Add the vectors by components.
  3. Zero-field point: lies outside the pair on the side of the smaller charge (same signs: between them).
Watch out
  • The field at a point does not depend on the test charge.

Fields of continuous charge distributions

Integrate $dE$ over the distribution; use symmetry to cancel components. Ring on axis, infinite line and infinite sheet are the standard results.

Key formulas
ring: $E=\dfrac{kQx}{(x^2+R^2)^{3/2}}$; line: $E=\dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r}$; sheet: $E=\dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$; disk on axis $E=\dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(1-\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+R^2}}\right)$

How it is asked: Field on the axis of a ring or disk; Field of a uniformly charged rod.

Fields of continuous charge distributions: method and traps

Method
  1. Split into charge elements $dq=\lambda\,dl$ or $\sigma\,dA$.
  2. Cancel components by symmetry; integrate the rest.
  3. Check limits: far away it looks like a point charge; very close to a sheet it becomes uniform.
Watch out
  • Which form of density: $\lambda$ (C/m), $\sigma$ (C/m²), $\rho$ (C/m³).

Charged particle in an electric field

A charge $q$ in a uniform field has constant acceleration $a=qE/m$ — treat it like projectile motion.

Key formulas
$a=\dfrac{qE}{m}$, $\ v^2=v_0^2+2ad$, $\ \Delta K=qE\,d=-q\Delta V$, deflection $y=\dfrac{qE}{2m}\left(\dfrac{L}{v_x}\right)^2$

How it is asked: Electron between two plates; Speed gained from a potential difference.

Charged particle in an electric field: method and traps

Method
  1. Find the force $qE$ and its direction (sign of $q$).
  2. Kinematics with $a=qE/m$ (or the work–energy theorem).
  3. Neglect gravity unless the problem gives it.
Watch out
  • Electron and proton accelerate in opposite directions.

Electric dipole

Two equal and opposite charges $\pm q$ a distance $d$ apart have dipole moment $p=qd$; in a field the torque is $\vec p\times\vec E$ and the energy $-\vec p\cdot\vec E$.

Key formulas
$p=qd$, $\ \tau=pE\sin\theta$, $\ U=-pE\cos\theta$, axis far field $E=\dfrac{2kp}{r^3}$, midpoint field $E=\dfrac{8kq}{d^2}$ (non-zero)

How it is asked: Torque on a dipole; Field at the midpoint.

Electric dipole: method and traps

Method
  1. Dipole moment points from $-$ to $+$.
  2. Torque or energy from the angle $\theta$ between $\vec p$ and $\vec E$.
  3. Midpoint: both fields point from $+$ to $-$ and add; the potential there is zero.
Watch out
  • Zero potential at the midpoint does not mean zero field.

Electric field lines of a dipole

$\pm1\,\mathrm{nC}$ at $x=\pm1\,\mathrm{cm}$. Lines leave the positive charge and end on the negative one; the field is strongest between and next to the charges, and at the midpoint it equals $2kq/a^2=1.8\times10^5\,\mathrm{N/C}$.

$$\vec E=k\sum_i q_i\frac{\vec r-\vec r_i}{|\vec r-\vec r_i|^3}$$

Field on the axis of a charged ring

$Q=5\,\mathrm{nC}$, $R=6\,\mathrm{cm}$: zero at the centre, a maximum at $z=R/\sqrt2=4.24\,\mathrm{cm}$ and an inverse-square tail $kQ/z^2$ far away. At $z=8\,\mathrm{cm}$ the field is $3.6\times10^3\,\mathrm{N/C}$.

$$E_z=\frac{kQz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}},\qquad E_{\max}\ \text{at}\ z=\frac{R}{\sqrt2}$$

How the field falls with distance for three charge distributions

Point charge $E\propto1/r^2$, long line of charge $E\propto1/r$, large sheet $E=\text{const}$. Log-log slopes: $-2$, $-1$ and $0$. (Each uses $1\,\mathrm{nC}$, $1\,\mathrm{nC/m}$ and $1\,\mathrm{nC/m^2}$.)

$$E_{\rm point}=\frac{kQ}{r^2},\qquad E_{\rm line}=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r},\qquad E_{\rm sheet}=\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$$

On the axis of a dipole: exact field and the far-field law

Charges $\pm q$ separated by $2a$: the exact axial field tends to $2kp/z^3$ (inverse cube) once $z\gg a$; the two curves differ by 2 % at $z=10a$ and by less than $1\,\%$ beyond $z=15a$.

$$E_{\rm exact}=kq\left[\frac1{(z-a)^2}-\frac1{(z+a)^2}\right]\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{2kp}{z^3},\quad p=2qa$$

Worked examples from the course problems

Worked example 1 · Charged ring and disk (Homework session 1, Chapter 2 Q4)
Charge is distributed uniformly around a ring of radius $R=2.40\,\mathrm{cm}$. At what distance from the ring's centre, along its axis, is the field magnitude greatest?
Show the answer and the solution

Answer: $1.7\,\mathrm{cm}$

$E(z)=\dfrac{kQz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}$. Set $\dfrac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}z}=0$: $(z^2+R^2)-3z^2=0\Rightarrow z=\dfrac{R}{\sqrt2}=1.70\,\mathrm{cm}$. A numerical maximisation of $E(z)$ confirms $z=1.70\,\mathrm{cm}$ (zero at the centre, and decaying as $1/z^2$ far away).
Worked example 2 · Charged particle in a field (Homework session 2, Q7)
In Millikan's experiment an oil drop of radius $1.64\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and density $0.851\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}$ is held suspended in a DOWNWARD electric field of $1.92\times10^{5}\,\mathrm{N/C}$. What is the magnitude of the charge on the drop in units of $e$?
Show the answer and the solution

Answer: $5\,\mathrm{e}$

Mass $m=\rho\,\tfrac43\pi r^3=851\times\tfrac43\pi(1.64\times10^{-6})^3=1.57\times10^{-14}\,\mathrm{kg}$. Equilibrium: $|q|E=mg\Rightarrow|q|=\dfrac{(1.57\times10^{-14})(9.8)}{1.92\times10^5}=8.0\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}=5.0\,e$. The force on the drop must point UP, opposite to the field, so the charge is negative: $q=-5e$.
Worked example 3 · Electric dipoles (Homework session 2, Q9)
How much work is required to turn an electric dipole through $180^\circ$ in a uniform field of $46.0\,\mathrm{N/C}$ if the dipole moment is $3.02\times10^{-25}\,\mathrm{C\,m}$ and the initial angle between $\vec p$ and $\vec E$ is $64^\circ$?
Show the answer and the solution

Answer: $1.22\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J}$

$U=-pE\cos\theta$. Initially $\theta=64^\circ$, finally $244^\circ$: $W=\Delta U=-pE(\cos244^\circ-\cos64^\circ)=2pE\cos64^\circ$. $W=2(3.02\times10^{-25})(46.0)\cos64^\circ=1.22\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J}$ (positive work is needed: the dipole prefers $\theta<90^\circ$).
Worked example 4 · Electric field and field lines (Homework session 1, Chapter 2 Q1(b))
Field lines on the left of a figure are twice as far apart as the lines on the right. If the field is $40\,\mathrm{N/C}$ on the left (at A), what is the field magnitude on the right (at B)?
Show the answer and the solution

Answer: $80\,\mathrm{N/C}$

The density of field lines (lines per unit area) is proportional to $E$. Lines twice as close means twice the density, so $E_B=2\times40=80\,\mathrm{N/C}$.
