Temperature scales and the zeroth law

Use kelvin in physics formulas: $T_K=T_C+273.15$; a temperature difference is the same in K and °C. Two bodies in equilibrium with a third are in equilibrium with each other.

Key formulas
$T_K=T_C+273.15$, $\ T_C=\tfrac59(T_F-32)$, $\ \Delta T(\mathrm K)=\Delta T(^\circ\mathrm C)$

How it is asked: Conversion °C ↔ K ↔ °F; Thermal equilibrium.

Temperature scales and the zeroth law: method and traps

Method
  1. Convert every temperature to kelvin before using a ratio or a gas law.
  2. For differences ($\Delta T$) kelvin and Celsius coincide.
  3. Equilibrium = same temperature, no net heat flow.
Watch out
  • Answers like $-87$ K are impossible: check the unit of the final answer.
  • Learning Support tip: convert to kelvin ($T_K=T_C+273.15$) whenever the temperature enters a gas-law formula; a temperature difference is the same in K and in $^\circ$C.

Thermal expansion

Lengths grow as $\Delta L=\alpha L\Delta T$ and volumes as $\Delta V=\beta V\Delta T$ with $\beta=3\alpha$.

Key formulas
$\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T$, $\ \Delta A=2\alpha A_0\Delta T$, $\ \Delta V=\beta V_0\Delta T$, $\ \beta=3\alpha$

How it is asked: Rod length change; Expansion gaps in a bridge.

Thermal expansion: method and traps

Method
  1. Identify linear, area or volume expansion.
  2. $\Delta T$ in K or °C (the same number).
  3. A hole expands like the material around it.
Watch out
  • Bimetal strips bend because $\alpha$ differs.
  • Learning Support tip: $\Delta T(\mathrm K)=\Delta T(^\circ\mathrm C)$, so $\Delta L=\alpha L\,\Delta T$ works with either; every material has its own $\alpha$.

Heat capacity, specific heat and calorimetry

$Q=mc\Delta T=C\Delta T$. In an insulated mixture heat lost equals heat gained.

Key formulas
$Q=mc\Delta T$, $\ \sum Q=0$, $\ c_{water}=4186\ \mathrm{J/kg\,K}=1\ \mathrm{cal/g\,K}$, $\ 1\ \mathrm{cal}=4.186$ J

How it is asked: Final temperature of a mixture; Heat to warm a sample.

Heat capacity, specific heat and calorimetry: method and traps

Method
  1. List every body, its mass, $c$, $T_i$.
  2. Write $\sum m_ic_i(T_f-T_i)=0$.
  3. Isolate $T_f$ (or the unknown $c$); check that $T_f$ lies between the initial temperatures.
Watch out
  • Mixing grams with kilograms.
  • Learning Support tip: specific heat appears in J/(kg K) or cal/(g K); convert calories to joules (1 cal = 4.186 J) before mixing data.

Latent heat and phase changes

A phase change at constant temperature absorbs $Q=mL$; add it to the $mc\Delta T$ of each heating stage.

Key formulas
$Q=mL_F$ (melting), $\ Q=mL_V$ (boiling), water: $L_F=333$ kJ/kg, $L_V=2256$ kJ/kg

How it is asked: Steam mixed with ice; Energy to turn ice into steam.

Latent heat and phase changes: method and traps

Method
  1. Draw the heating curve: warm, melt, warm, boil.
  2. Add $mc\Delta T$ for each warming stage and $mL$ for each change of state.
  3. Mixtures: heat lost by steam/water = heat gained by ice/water.
Watch out
  • Forgetting that ice must melt before it warms.
  • Learning Support tip: latent heat is only the heat of a change of phase; when a body merely warms up or cools down, use $Q=mc\Delta T$, not $mL$.

First law of thermodynamics

$\Delta E_{int}=Q-W$ with $Q>0$ when the system absorbs heat and $W>0$ when the system does work. Internal energy is a state function.

Key formulas
$\Delta E_{int}=Q-W$; adiabatic $Q=0$; isochoric $W=0$; cycle $\Delta E_{int}=0$ so $Q=W$; free expansion $Q=W=0$

How it is asked: Heat in one leg of a cycle; Internal energy change along another path; Sign of work and heat.

First law of thermodynamics: method and traps

Method
  1. Fix the sign convention: heat absorbed positive, work done by the gas positive.
  2. Use the special-case table (adiabatic, isochoric, cycle, free expansion).
  3. For two paths between the same states $\Delta E_{int}$ is equal.
Watch out
  • Work done on the gas is $-W$.
  • Q and W depend on the path, $\Delta E_{int}$ does not.
  • Learning Support tip: read the words absorbed and released: $Q>0$ when the system absorbs heat, $Q<0$ when it releases heat; $W>0$ when the system does the work.

Work by a gas and p–V diagrams

$W=\int p\,dV$ is the signed area under the process on a $p$–$V$ diagram; a clockwise cycle has positive net work equal to the enclosed area.

Key formulas
isobaric $W=p\Delta V$; isochoric $W=0$; isothermal $W=nRT\ln\dfrac{V_f}{V_i}$; adiabatic $W=\dfrac{p_iV_i-p_fV_f}{\gamma-1}$

How it is asked: Work of an isothermal expansion; Net heat in a p–V cycle; Adiabatic compression work.

Work by a gas and p–V diagrams: method and traps

Method
  1. Identify the process type of each leg from the diagram.
  2. Compute $W$ as an area (rectangle, trapezoid) or with the formula; sign from the direction of $V$.
  3. Cycle: net work = enclosed area, clockwise positive.
Watch out
  • No work on a vertical (isochoric) segment.
  • $W=p(V_f-V_i)$ only if $p$ is constant.

Heating curve of 1 kg of water from ice at −20 °C to steam at 120 °C

Slopes are set by the specific heats (ice $2090$, water $4186$, steam $2010\,\mathrm{J/kg\,K}$); the plateaus are the latent heats of fusion ($334\,\mathrm{kJ}$) and vaporisation ($2256\,\mathrm{kJ}$). Total heat: $3090.6\,\mathrm{kJ}$.

$$Q=mc\,\Delta T\ \ (\text{no phase change}),\qquad Q=mL\ \ (\text{phase change})$$

Thermal expansion of 1 m rods

$\Delta L=\alpha L\,\Delta T$: for the same temperature rise aluminium ($23\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$) grows almost twice as much as steel ($12\times10^{-6}$), copper ($17\times10^{-6}$) and Pyrex glass ($3.2\times10^{-6}$) far less.

$$\Delta L=\alpha L\,\Delta T,\qquad \Delta A\approx2\alpha A\,\Delta T,\qquad \Delta V\approx3\alpha V\,\Delta T$$

Calorimetry: final temperature of a mixture

Hot liquid at $80^\circ\mathrm{C}$ poured into cold water at $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$. With the same specific heat the temperature is a mass-weighted average; oil ($c=2000\,\mathrm{J/kg\,K}$) gives up less heat per kilogram, so the mixture stays cooler.

$$m_hc_h(T_h-T_f)=m_cc_c(T_f-T_c)\ \Rightarrow\ T_f=\frac{m_hc_hT_h+m_cc_cT_c}{m_hc_h+m_cc_c}$$

Work on a p-V diagram: isothermal and isobaric expansion

$0.10\,\mathrm{mol}$ of ideal gas at $300\,\mathrm{K}$ expands from $2\,\mathrm{L}$ to $6\,\mathrm{L}$. The work is the area under the path: $nRT\ln3=274\,\mathrm{J}$ along the isotherm, $p_1\Delta V=499\,\mathrm{J}$ at constant pressure.

$$W=\int p\,dV,\qquad W_{\rm iso}=nRT\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1},\qquad W_{\rm isobar}=p\,\Delta V$$

Worked examples from the course problems

Worked example 1 · Thermal expansion (Thermodynamics tutorial Part I, Q1)
At $20^\circ\mathrm{C}$ a rod is exactly $20.05\,\mathrm{cm}$ long on a steel ruler. Both are placed in an oven at $270^\circ\mathrm{C}$, where the rod measures $20.11\,\mathrm{cm}$ on the same ruler. The coefficient of linear expansion of steel is $11\times10^{-6}\,/^\circ\mathrm{C}$. What is the coefficient of linear expansion of the rod?
Show the answer and the solution

Answer: $2.3\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{/^\circ\,C}$

The ruler has expanded too, so $20.11$ 'cm' on the hot ruler is longer than $20.11$ true cm: the true length is $20.11(1+11\times10^{-6}\times250)=20.165\,\mathrm{cm}$. $\alpha=\dfrac{\Delta L}{L_0\Delta T}=\dfrac{20.165-20.05}{20.05\times250}=2.3\times10^{-5}\,/^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Shortcut: $\alpha_{\text{rod}}\approx\alpha_{\text{apparent}}+\alpha_{\text{steel}}=1.2\times10^{-5}+1.1\times10^{-5}$.
Worked example 2 · Specific heat and calorimetry (Thermodynamics tutorial Part I, Q3)
What mass of steam at $100^\circ\mathrm{C}$ must be mixed with $150\,\mathrm{g}$ of ice at its melting point, in a thermally insulated container, to produce liquid water at $50^\circ\mathrm{C}$? ($L_f=333\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$, $L_v=2256\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$, $c_w=4186\,\mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$)
Show the answer and the solution

Answer: $33\,\mathrm{g}$

Heat the ice needs: melt $0.150(333\,\mathrm{kJ})=49.95\,\mathrm{kJ}$ and warm the meltwater $0.150(4186)(50)=31.4\,\mathrm{kJ}$; total $81.3\,\mathrm{kJ}$. Heat each kg of steam gives: condense $2256\,\mathrm{kJ}$ and cool $4186(50)=209\,\mathrm{kJ}$: $2465\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}$. $m=\dfrac{81.3}{2465}=0.0330\,\mathrm{kg}=33.0\,\mathrm{g}$.
Worked example 3 · Work and the first law (Thermodynamics tutorial Part I, Q4(a))
A gas expands from $V_0=1.0\,\mathrm{m^3}$ to $4.0V_0$ while its pressure falls from $p_0=40\,\mathrm{Pa}$ to $p_0/4$. Path A: expansion at constant pressure $p_0$, then pressure drop at constant volume. How much work does the gas do along path A?
Show the answer and the solution

Answer: $120\,\mathrm{J}$

Work by the gas = area under the path in the $p$-$V$ diagram. Path A (expansion at $40\,\mathrm{Pa}$, then an isochoric pressure drop): $W=40\times3.0=120\,\mathrm{J}$. Path B (straight line): $W=\tfrac12(40+10)\times3.0=75\,\mathrm{J}$. Path C (pressure drop at $1.0\,\mathrm{m^3}$ first, then expansion at $10\,\mathrm{Pa}$): $W=10\times3.0=30\,\mathrm{J}$.
Worked example 4 · First law (Thermodynamics tutorial Part I, Q7)
Suppose $200\,\mathrm{J}$ of work is done ON a system and $70.0\,\mathrm{cal}$ is extracted from it as heat. With $W$ the work done BY the system, what is $\Delta E_{\text{int}}$ (in joules)?
Show the answer and the solution

Answer: $-93\,\mathrm{J}$

$W=-200\,\mathrm{J}$ (work done on the system is negative work by it); $Q=-70.0\,\mathrm{cal}=-293\,\mathrm{J}$ (heat leaving). $\Delta E_{\text{int}}=Q-W=-293-(-200)=-93\,\mathrm{J}$.
