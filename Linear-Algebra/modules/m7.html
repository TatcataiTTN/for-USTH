After this module you can

  • Compute $A^k$ via $PD^kP^{-1}$.
  • Orthogonally diagonalize a symmetric matrix and use the standard proofs.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

  • $A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$, $\mathbf v\ne0$. Eigenvalues are the roots of $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$; eigenvectors for $\lambda$: $\ker(A-\lambda I)\setminus\{0\}$.
  • $\sum\lambda_i=\operatorname{tr}A$ and $\prod\lambda_i=\det A$ (use them to check your work).
  • Triangular matrices: the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries.
  • Algebraic multiplicity (root multiplicity) $\ge$ geometric multiplicity $=\dim\ker(A-\lambda I)$.

Diagonalization: the recipe

  • 1. $\det(A-\lambda I)$, factor it. 2. For each $\lambda$ find a basis of $\ker(A-\lambda I)$.
  • 3. $A$ is diagonalizable iff the total number of independent eigenvectors is $n$ (geometric $=$ algebraic multiplicity for every $\lambda$).
  • 4. $P$ = eigenvectors as columns, $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i)$ in the same order: $P^{-1}AP=D$.
  • Not diagonalizable: $\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\1&2&1\\0&-1&1\end{bmatrix}$ has $\lambda=1$ twice but only a one-dimensional eigenspace.

Powers of a matrix

$$A=PDP^{-1}\ \Rightarrow\ A^k=PD^kP^{-1}$$
  • $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&3\\2&0\end{bmatrix}$ has $\lambda=3,-2$: $(A^n)_{11}=\frac{3\cdot3^n+2(-2)^n}{5}$.
  • Final 2022–23: $A=\begin{bmatrix}2&0&-2\\0&3&0\\0&0&3\end{bmatrix}$ gives $A^k$ with entries $2^k,\ 3^k$ and $2\cdot2^k-2\cdot3^k$.
  • Check the formula at $k=1$ (it must return $A$).

Symmetric matrices

  • Spectral theorem: a real symmetric matrix is orthogonally diagonalizable: $P^TAP=D$ with $P$ orthogonal ($P^{-1}=P^T$).
  • Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are already orthogonal; inside a repeated eigenvalue orthogonalise with Gram–Schmidt, then normalise.
  • Example: $\begin{bmatrix}2&-2&2\\-2&5&1\\2&1&5\end{bmatrix}$ has $\lambda=0,6,6$.
  • Then $A^k=PD^kP^T$.

Worked examples from the course problems

Worked example 1 · Eigenvalues (Exercise Sheet 7, Ex. 3)
For $A=\begin{bmatrix}2 & -1 & 1 \\ -2 & 3 & -2 \\ -1 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, the eigenvalue $\lambda=1$ has algebraic multiplicity 2. What is the dimension of its eigenspace?
Show the answer and the solution

Answer: $2$

$\dim\ker(A-1I)=n-\operatorname{rank}(A-1I)=3-1=2$.
Worked example 2 · Powers via diagonalization (Exercise Sheet 7, Ex. 8)
For $A=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 \\ 2 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ (eigenvalues $3$ and $-2$), the $(1,1)$ entry of $A^5$ is
Show the answer and the solution

Answer: $133$

$A=PDP^{-1}$ gives $A^n=PD^nP^{-1}$; here $(A^n)_{11}=\frac{2 \left(-2\right)^{n}}{5} + \frac{3 \cdot 3^{n}}{5}$. For $n=5$: $A^5=\begin{bmatrix}133 & 165 \\ 110 & 78\end{bmatrix}$, so the entry is $133$.
Worked example 3 · Eigenvectors (Exercise Sheet 7, Ex. 3)
For $A=\begin{bmatrix}2 & -1 & 1 \\ -2 & 3 & -2 \\ -1 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$, which vector is an eigenvector for $\lambda=3$?
Show the answer and the solution

Answer: $(-1, 2, 1)$

Solve $(A-3I)x=0$. Then $Ax=\lambda x$: $A(-1, 2, 1)=(-3, 6, 3)=3\cdot(-1, 2, 1)$.
Worked example 4 · Diagonalization (Linear Algebra Summary (Gen 16), VII.2 (sufficient condition))
An $n\times n$ matrix has $n$ distinct eigenvalues. What follows?
Show the answer and the solution

Answer: it is diagonalizable; the converse is false (e.g. $I_3$ is diagonalizable with the single eigenvalue $1$)

Eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent, so $n$ distinct eigenvalues give $n$ independent eigenvectors: a sufficient condition. It is not necessary: $I_3$ has one repeated eigenvalue but is diagonalizable, whereas $\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ (repeated eigenvalue, only one eigenvector direction) is not.
